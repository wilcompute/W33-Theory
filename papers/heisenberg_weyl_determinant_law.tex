\begin{abstract}
\noindent Let $R$ be a finite Frobenius ring of odd residue characteristic
whose generating character $\psi$ has order $p$ --- for example a finite field
$\FF_q$, a truncated polynomial ring $\FF_p[x]/(x^k)$, or the noncommutative
$M_2(\FF_p)$ --- and write $q=|R|$. Let $H$ be
the Heisenberg group of order $q^3$ over $R$ and $\psi_t$ a nontrivial
character of its centre. An \emph{inverse-closed section} $c$ assigns to each
nonzero $v\in R^2$ a central coordinate $c(v)\in R$ with $c(-v)=-c(v)$; the
associated \emph{Weyl block} $B_t(c)$ is the image, in the $q$-dimensional
representation attached to $\psi_t$, of the sum of the corresponding group
elements. We prove that the first-order variation of $\det B_t(c)$ about the
zero section is divisible by $\lambda^{\vlam(q)+2}$, where $\lambda=1-\zeta_p$,
and that the exponent splits canonically as
\[
\vlam(q) \;+\; 2,\qquad
\underbrace{\vlam(q)}_{\text{ramification of }q}
\;+\;\underbrace{2}_{\text{inverse closure}} .
\]
The proof rests on a closed formula
$\tr\bigl(\adj(F)\,D\bigr)=\det(F)\,q\,S/(q^2-1)$ in which $F$ is the block of
the zero section and $S=\sum_{v}(\zeta_q^{-tc(v)}-1)$; the block $F$ is shown to
satisfy $F^2+2F-(q^2-1)I=0$ by a symplectic character sum. We then prove the
congruence itself, in the form
\[
\det B_t(c)\;\equiv\;\det F \pmod{\lambda^{\,\vlam(q)+4}},
\]
valid for every such $R$ and every section, modulo one explicitly identified
lemma on the top exterior power. Here $\vlam(q)=\log_p(q)\,(p-1)$, so the
exponent is $q+3$ when $R=\FF_q$ with $q$ prime, and $8$ when $|R|=9$. The
constant $4$ splits as $2+2$: two units of vanishing from inverse closure at
first order, and two more from a cancellation between the first two orders
whose mechanism is that a \emph{symmetric} coefficient is paired against the
\emph{antisymmetric} symplectic form. Neither summand depends on the structure
of $R$; only the ramification does. Several steps of the argument would fail on
valuation counting alone, and each is rescued by inverse closure. The law is
insensitive to whether $R$ is a field: $\FF_{p^k}$ and $\FF_p[x]/(x^k)$ give
the same exponent, while $\ZZ/p^2$ --- whose generating character has order
$p^2$ --- does not satisfy the law at all.

The congruence reduces to an identity about the power sums $\tr(D^m)$, and the
main structural result of the paper concerns those. Expanding $\tr(D^m)$ over
$m$-tuples of total displacement zero and grouping by cyclic orbit gives
classes $S_d$ indexed by the divisors of $m$. We prove a \emph{sieve theorem}:
if $e$ denotes the order of the generating character and $t\mid m$ satisfies
$m/t$ odd and $e\mid(m/t)$, then
\[
\sum_{d\mid t} d\,S_d \;=\; q\Bigl(\sum_{v\neq0}d_v^{\,m/t}\Bigr)^{t}\;=\;0 .
\]
Two apparently separate phenomena are its corollaries. When $m$ is odd the
index set of relations is downward closed, M\"obius inversion runs, and each
$S_d$ with $e\mid(m/d)$ vanishes identically; when $m$ is even it is not, and
the theorem degenerates to a single relation. The sieve system is triangular
along divisibility, so its rank is the number of relations and the number of
unconstrained classes is $\tau(m)-\#\{u\mid m: u\text{ odd},\ e\mid u\}$; this
equals $1$ precisely when $m$ is a power of $p$. Finally $d\,S_d$ has the
closed form $q\sum_{c\mid d}\mu(d/c)\bigl(\sum_v d_v^{\,m/c}\bigr)^{c}$, from
which the sieve theorem follows in two lines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Let $R$ be a finite commutative local Frobenius ring of odd residue
characteristic $p$ whose generating character $\psi$ has order $p$, and put
$q=|R|$. Two families of examples run through the paper: the finite fields
$R=\FF_q$, and the truncated polynomial rings $R=\FF_p[x]/(x^k)$, whose socle
is $(x^{k-1})$ and whose generating character is $\psi(c)=\zeta_p^{c_{k-1}}$.
The reader who prefers may take $R=\FF_q$ throughout; nothing in the argument
uses invertibility of nonzero elements, and Section~\ref{sec:pf} shows the
distinction is invisible to the result. The hypothesis is not ad hoc: by Wood's
theorem \cite{Wood} the finite Frobenius rings are precisely the finite rings
for which the MacWilliams extension theorem holds, and a generating character
is the standard tool in that theory, so the setting here is the usual one of
ring-linear coding theory. What our argument additionally requires --- and what
fails over $\ZZ/p^2$ --- is only that the generating character have order $p$.
Set
\[
H=\bigl\{(a,b,c):a,b,c\in R\bigr\},\qquad
(a,b,c)(a',b',c')=(a+a',\,b+b',\,c+c'-ab'+a'b),
\]
a group of order $q^3$ with centre $Z=\{(0,0,c)\}$; for $R=\FF_q$ it is the
extraspecial group of exponent $q$. Each nontrivial character $\psi_t(0,0,c)=\zeta^{tc}$
($\zeta=e^{2\pi i/q}$, $t\not\equiv0$) determines a $q$-dimensional irreducible
representation $\rho_t$ of $H$, realized on $\mathbb C^{\FF_q}$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho}
\rho_t(a,b,c)\,e_x \;=\; \zeta^{\,t(c+2xb+ab)}\,e_{x+a}.
\end{equation}

Call a function $c:\FF_q^2\setminus\{0\}\to\FF_q$ an \emph{inverse-closed
section} if $c(-v)=-c(v)$; equivalently, the set
$S(c)=\{(v,c(v)):v\neq0\}\subset H$ is closed under inversion, since
$(a,b,z)^{-1}=(-a,-b,-z)$. Such sections index the ways of lifting the
punctured plane $\FF_q^2\setminus\{0\}$ into $H$ compatibly with inversion,
and the object of interest is the \emph{Weyl block}
\[
B_t(c)\;=\;\sum_{v\in\FF_q^2\setminus\{0\}}\rho_t\bigl(v,c(v)\bigr)
\;\in\; M_q\bigl(\ZZ[\zeta]\bigr),
\]
a Hermitian matrix over the cyclotomic ring $\ZZ[\zeta]$. Write $F=B_t(0)$ for
the block of the zero (\emph{flat}) section.

Sections arise naturally: $S(c)$ together with $Z\setminus\{1\}$ is the Cayley
set of a strongly regular graph on $H$ with parameters
$\bigl(q^3,(q-1)(q+2),q-2,q+2\bigr)$ exactly when $c$ is flat up to the obvious
symmetries, and the family of these partial difference sets in $H$ is
classical \cite{Ma,PDSS}. The determinants $\det B_t(c)$, as $c$ ranges over
all sections, are therefore a natural arithmetic invariant of that family. Our
results describe how little they vary.

Throughout, $\lambda=1-\zeta$ is the unique prime of $\ZZ[\zeta]$ above $q$, so
that $(q)=(\lambda)^{q-1}$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vq}
\vlam(q)=q-1,
\end{equation}
and $\vlam$ denotes the $\lambda$-adic valuation.

\begin{theorem}[first-order law]\label{thm:main}
Let $q$ be an odd prime, $t\not\equiv0$, and $c$ an inverse-closed section. Put
$D=B_t(c)-F$ and $T_1=\tr\bigl(\adj(F)\,D\bigr)$, the first-order term of
$\det(F+D)$. Then
\[
T_1\;=\;\frac{\det(F)\;q\;S}{q^2-1},
\qquad
S=\sum_{v\in\FF_q^2\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(\zeta^{-t c(v)}-1\bigr),
\]
and consequently
\[
\vlam(T_1)\;=\;(q-1)+\vlam(S)\;\ge\;(q-1)+2\;=\;q+1 .
\]
\end{theorem}

The two summands are structurally distinct: $q-1=\vlam(q)$ is the ramification
index \eqref{eq:vq}, while the $2$ comes from inverse closure through the
factorization $w+w^{-1}-2=-(1-w)(1-w^{-1})$ applied to $w=\zeta^{tc(v)}$. Only
the first depends on $q$ being prime; writing the bound as $\vlam(q)+2$ makes
the statement correct for every prime power (Section~\ref{sec:pf}).

Section~\ref{sec:sharp} proves the congruence at exponent $\vlam(q)+2$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cong}) and then lifts it to the sharp $\vlam(q)+4$
(Theorems~\ref{thm:cancel} and \ref{thm:sharp}), modulo a single explicitly
identified bound on the top exterior power. Section~\ref{sec:pf} explains why
no separate prime-power statement is needed.

\subsection{How the results depend on one another}\label{sec:roadmap}

The sections below follow the order in which the results were found, which is
not the order in which they depend on each other. A reader who wants the
logical structure should take it as follows.

At the root is a single lemma about one power sum. Writing
$\mathrm{Ps}(k)=\sum_{v\neq0}d_v^{\,k}$ and letting $e$ be the order of the
generating character,
\[
\mathrm{Ps}(k)=0 \text{ for every inverse-closed section}
\iff k \text{ is odd and } e\mid k .
\tag{$\ast$}
\]
Inverse closure pairs $v$ with $-v$, so $\mathrm{Ps}(k)$ is a sum of terms
$2\operatorname{Re}(u-1)^{k}$, and $(u-1)^k$ is purely imaginary exactly under
those conditions; the one-pair section of Proposition~\ref{prop:converse1}
supplies the converse.

Everything else descends from $(\ast)$. The closed form
(Proposition~\ref{prop:closed}) writes each cyclic class $d\,S_d$ in terms of
$\mathrm{Ps}$ alone; summing it over $d\mid t$ and exchanging the order gives
the sieve theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:sieve}), whose right-hand side is
$q\,\mathrm{Ps}(m/t)^{t}$ and therefore vanishes by $(\ast)$. The sieve in turn
yields the odd-class vanishing theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass}) when its
index set is downward closed, and a single propagation relation when it is not;
its system is triangular along divisibility, which gives the rank count
(Proposition~\ref{prop:rank}) and hence
$\mathrm{free}(m)=1\iff m$ is a power of $p$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:freeone}).
The determinant congruence of \S\ref{sec:sharp}, which motivated all of this,
sits at the other end: it follows from the factorial law, which is an
assertion about the very power sums $(\ast)$ constrains.

One boundary is worth stating at the outset. The closed form requires
$e\mid(m/d)$, and the top class $d=m$ never satisfies it; the reason is
identified in \S\ref{sec:closedform}, and it is a genuine feature of the object
rather than a defect of the argument.

\section{The flat block}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:trace}
For every noncentral $g\in H$ and every $t\not\equiv0$, $\tr\rho_t(g)=0$.
Consequently $\tr F=0$ and $\tr B_t(c)=0$ for every section $c$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $g=(a,b,c)$. If $a\neq0$ then by \eqref{eq:rho} the matrix $\rho_t(g)$
moves every basis vector $e_x$ to $e_{x+a}\neq e_x$, so its diagonal vanishes.
If $a=0$ and $b\neq0$ then $\rho_t(0,b,c)$ is diagonal with entries
$\zeta^{t(c+2xb)}$, and
$\tr\rho_t(0,b,c)=\zeta^{tc}\sum_{x\in\FF_q}\zeta^{2tbx}=0$
because $2tb\neq0$ in $\FF_q$ ($q$ is odd). Every element of $S(c)$ is
noncentral, whence the last claim.
\end{proof}

Let $\omega(v,w)=ab'-a'b$ denote the symplectic form on $\FF_q^2$, where
$v=(a,b)$ and $w=(a',b')$, so that $(v,0)(w,0)=(v+w,\,-\omega(v,w))$.

\begin{lemma}[flat block]\label{lem:flat}
$F^2+2F-(q^2-1)I=0$. Hence $F$ has spectrum
$\{(q-1)^{(q+1)/2},\,(-(q+1))^{(q-1)/2}\}$,
\[
\det F=(q-1)^{(q+1)/2}\bigl(-(q+1)\bigr)^{(q-1)/2},
\qquad
F^{-1}=\frac{F+2I}{q^2-1},
\]
and $\det F$ is a unit at $\lambda$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Expanding and using $\rho_t(u,z)=\zeta^{tz}\rho_t(u,0)$,
\[
F^2=\sum_{v,w\neq0}\rho_t\bigl((v,0)(w,0)\bigr)
    =\sum_{v,w\neq0}\zeta^{-t\,\omega(v,w)}\,\rho_t(v+w,0).
\]
Split according to $u=v+w$. The terms with $u=0$ have $w=-v$ and
$\omega(v,-v)=0$, contributing $(q^2-1)I$. For fixed $u\neq0$ the constraint is
$v\notin\{0,u\}$, and $\omega(v,u-v)=\omega(v,u)$, so those terms contribute
\[
\Bigl(\sum_{v\neq0,u}\zeta^{-t\,\omega(v,u)}\Bigr)\rho_t(u,0).
\]
Since $u\neq0$, the map $v\mapsto\omega(v,u)$ is a nonzero linear functional on
$\FF_q^2$, so $\sum_{v\in\FF_q^2}\zeta^{-t\omega(v,u)}=0$; removing the two
excluded values $v=0$ and $v=u$, both of which give $\omega=0$, leaves
$-2$. Summing over $u\neq0$ gives $-2F$, so $F^2=(q^2-1)I-2F$.

Thus the minimal polynomial of $F$ divides
$x^2+2x-(q^2-1)=(x-(q-1))(x+(q+1))$, so the eigenvalues lie in
$\{q-1,-(q+1)\}$. If they occur with multiplicities $\alpha,\beta$ then
$\alpha+\beta=q$ and, by Lemma~\ref{lem:trace},
$\alpha(q-1)-\beta(q+1)=0$; solving gives $\alpha=(q+1)/2$,
$\beta=(q-1)/2$. The remaining assertions follow, the last because $q\pm1$ are
prime to $q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Lemma~\ref{lem:flat} also reproves the two ``trace laws''
$\tr F=0$ and $\tr F^2=q(q^2-1)$; the latter is
$(q-1)^2\frac{q+1}{2}+(q+1)^2\frac{q-1}{2}=q(q^2-1)$. These hold for every
section, not only the flat one, and are used below.
\end{remark}

\section{The first-order law}

\begin{lemma}[pairing]\label{lem:pair}
Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $w\in R$ a unit with inverse $w^{-1}$. Then
\[
w+w^{-1}-2=-(1-w)(1-w^{-1}).
\]
In particular, if $\mu\mid(1-w)$ and $\mu\mid(1-w^{-1})$ then
$\mu^2\mid\bigl(w+w^{-1}-2\bigr)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$(1-w)(1-w^{-1})=1-w-w^{-1}+ww^{-1}=2-w-w^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Since $\det F$ is a $\lambda$-unit, $F$ is invertible and
$T_1=\tr(\adj(F)D)=\det(F)\tr(F^{-1}D)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:flat},
$F^{-1}=(F+2I)/(q^2-1)$, and $\tr D=\tr B_t(c)-\tr F=0$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:trace}, so
\[
T_1=\frac{\det(F)}{q^2-1}\Bigl[\tr(FD)+2\tr D\Bigr]
   =\frac{\det(F)\,\tr(FD)}{q^2-1}.
\]
Now $\tr(FD)=\tr\bigl(FB_t(c)\bigr)-\tr(F^2)=\tr\bigl(FB_t(c)\bigr)-q(q^2-1)$.
For the first term,
\[
\tr\bigl(FB_t(c)\bigr)
=\sum_{v,w\neq0}\tr\rho_t\bigl((v,0)(w,c(w))\bigr),
\qquad
(v,0)\bigl(w,c(w)\bigr)=\bigl(v+w,\;c(w)-\omega(v,w)\bigr).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:trace} only central products contribute, i.e.\ only $w=-v$;
then $\omega(v,-v)=0$ and the product is $\bigl(0,0,c(-v)\bigr)=(0,0,-c(v))$,
whose trace is $q\,\zeta^{-tc(v)}$. Hence
$\tr(FB_t(c))=q\sum_{v\neq0}\zeta^{-tc(v)}$, and since there are $q^2-1$
nonzero $v$,
\[
\tr(FD)=q\sum_{v\neq0}\bigl(\zeta^{-tc(v)}-1\bigr)=q\,S,
\]
which is the displayed formula.

For the valuation, $\vlam(\det F)=\vlam(q^2-1)=0$ and $\vlam(q)=q-1$ by
\eqref{eq:vq}, so $\vlam(T_1)=(q-1)+\vlam(S)$. Group the sum defining $S$ into
the $(q^2-1)/2$ pairs $\{v,-v\}$. Using $c(-v)=-c(v)$ and
Lemma~\ref{lem:pair} with $w=\zeta^{tc(v)}$,
\[
\bigl(\zeta^{-tc(v)}-1\bigr)+\bigl(\zeta^{tc(v)}-1\bigr)
=-\bigl(1-\zeta^{tc(v)}\bigr)\bigl(1-\zeta^{-tc(v)}\bigr).
\]
Each factor is $0$ if $c(v)=0$ and has valuation $1$ otherwise, so every pair
contributes valuation $2$ or $\infty$; therefore $\vlam(S)\ge2$ and
$\vlam(T_1)\ge q+1$.
\end{proof}

\section{The congruence}\label{sec:sharp}

Write $G=F^{-1}D$, $p_m=\tr(G^m)$, and $e_k=e_k(G)$, so that
$\det B_t(c)=\det(F)\bigl(1+\sum_{k\ge1}e_k\bigr)$. Note $G$ is
$\lambda$-integral, since $F^{-1}=(F+2I)/(q^2-1)$ and $q^2-1$ is prime to
$\lambda$; hence so is every $e_k$.

\begin{lemma}[power sums]\label{lem:power}
$\vlam(p_m)\ge q+1$ for every $m\ge1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $H=(F+2I)D$, so $p_m=\tr(H^m)/(q^2-1)^m$ and
$\vlam(p_m)=\vlam(\tr H^m)$. Both $F$ and $D$ are $\ZZ[\zeta]$-linear
combinations of the matrices $\rho_t(g)$, and $D=\sum_{v\neq0}d_v\rho_t(v,0)$
with $d_v=\zeta^{tc(v)}-1$, each of valuation $\ge1$. Expanding $\tr(H^m)$
therefore produces monomials
$d_{v_1}\cdots d_{v_m}\cdot\tr\rho_t(g)$ for single group elements $g$, with
exactly $m$ factors $d$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:trace} such a trace vanishes unless
$g$ is central, in which case it equals $q$ times a root of unity. Every
surviving monomial thus carries one factor $q$ and $m$ factors $d$, so
$\vlam(\tr H^m)\ge(q-1)+m$, which is $\ge q+1$ for $m\ge2$.

For $m=1$ this count gives only $q$. But by the computation in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, $\tr H=\tr(FD)+2\tr D=qS$, and inverse closure gives
$\vlam(S)\ge2$; hence $\vlam(p_1)=(q-1)+\vlam(S)\ge q+1$ as well.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[top exterior power]\label{lem:top}
$\vlam(e_q)\ge q+1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$e_q=\det G=\det D/\det F$ and $\vlam(\det F)=0$, so it suffices that
$\vlam(\det D)\ge q+1$. Every entry of $D$ is a $\ZZ[\zeta]$-combination of the
$d_v$, so $D=\lambda D'$ with $D'$ $\lambda$-integral and
$\det D=\lambda^{q}\det D'$; we must show $\det D'\equiv0\pmod\lambda$.

Reduce modulo $\lambda$, identifying $\ZZ[\zeta]/\lambda=\FF_q$ via
$\zeta\mapsto1$. Then $(\zeta^{k}-1)/(1-\zeta)\equiv-k$ and
$\rho_t(v,0)\equiv P_{a_v}$, where $P_a$ is the permutation matrix of
$x\mapsto x+a$; grouping the $q^2-1$ vectors by their first coordinate,
\[
D'\;\equiv\;\sum_{a\in\FF_q}\kappa_a P_a \pmod\lambda,
\qquad
\kappa_a=-t\sum_{b}c(a,b).
\]
The $P_a$ span the group algebra
$\FF_q[\ZZ/q]=\FF_q[y]/(y^{q}-1)=\FF_q[y]/\bigl((y-1)^{q}\bigr)$, in which the
determinant of multiplication by $f(y)$ is $f(1)^{q}$. Hence
$\det D'\equiv\bigl(\sum_a\kappa_a\bigr)^{q}
=\bigl(-t\sum_{v\neq0}c(v)\bigr)^{q}$, and inverse closure makes
$\sum_{v\neq0}c(v)=0$ because the terms cancel in pairs $\{v,-v\}$. So
$\det D'\equiv0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the congruence]\label{thm:cong}
For every odd prime power $q$, every $t\not\equiv0$, and every inverse-closed
section $c$,
\[
\det B_t(c)\;\equiv\;\det F\pmod{\lambda^{\,\vlam(q)+2}} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Newton's identities, $k\,e_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i-1}e_{k-i}p_i$. For
$1\le k\le q-1$ the integer $k$ is a unit at $\lambda$, so induction on $k$
together with Lemma~\ref{lem:power} and the $\lambda$-integrality of the
$e_j$ gives $\vlam(e_k)\ge q+1$. For $k=q$ the same recursion would lose
$\vlam(q)=q-1$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:top} supplies that case directly. Hence
$\vlam(e_k)\ge q+1$ for all $k\ge1$ and
$\det B_t(c)-\det F=\det(F)\sum_{k\ge1}e_k$ has valuation $\ge q+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Both places where valuation counting alone is insufficient --- the first power
sum in Lemma~\ref{lem:power} and the top exterior power in
Lemma~\ref{lem:top} --- are rescued by inverse closure, in the first case
through $\vlam(S)\ge2$ and in the second through $\sum_{v}c(v)=0$. Without
that hypothesis the argument gives only $\lambda^{q}$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The sharp exponent}

The exponent can be improved by exactly two, and the improvement is a single
cancellation which we now prove.

\begin{lemma}[parity]\label{lem:parity}
For odd $m$, $\vlam(p_m)\ge\vlam(q)+m+1$. In particular
$\vlam(p_3)\ge\vlam(q)+4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:power} the coefficient of the leading power of
$\lambda$ in $\tr(H^m)$ is, up to a unit, a sum over $m$-tuples $(v_1,\dots,v_m)$
of total displacement zero of $m_{v_1}\cdots m_{v_m}$, where $d_v\equiv-m_v\lambda$
and $m_v=\psi$-exponent of $c(v)$. The involution
$(v_1,\dots,v_m)\mapsto(-v_1,\dots,-v_m)$ preserves the constraint, and
$c(-v)=-c(v)$ sends each $m_{v_i}$ to $-m_{v_i}$, multiplying the summand by
$(-1)^m=-1$. So the sum equals its own negative and vanishes in the residue
field, gaining one further power of $\lambda$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[the two closed forms]\label{lem:closed}
Put $Q=\sum_{v,w\neq0}d_vd_w\,\psi_t\bigl(-\omega(v,w)\bigr)$ and
$R=\tr(FDFD)/q$. Then, exactly,
\[
\tr H=qS,\quad \tr(D^2)=-2qS,\quad \tr(FD^2)=q\,(Q+2S),\quad
\tr(H^2)=qR+4qQ,
\]
and, modulo $\lambda^{4}$, $\;Q\equiv0\;$ and $\;R\equiv-2S$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The exact identities follow as in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, keeping track of
which products of $\rho$'s are central. For $Q$: expanding
$\psi_t(-\omega)\equiv1+\text{(linear in }\lambda)$ and using
$d_vd_w\equiv m_vm_w\lambda^2$, the constant term is
$\sum_{v,w}d_vd_w=S^2$, of valuation $\ge4$ since $\vlam(S)\ge2$; the surviving
term pairs the symmetric coefficient $d_vd_w$ against the antisymmetric
$\omega(v,w)$ and therefore cancels under $(v,w)\mapsto(w,v)$. For $R$:
reparametrizing so that the two free vectors are summed for fixed $(v,w)$, and
writing $s=-(v+w)$, the phase is $2\omega(x,v)+\omega(x,s)+\omega(v,s)$; summing
over the $q^2-2$ admissible $x$ and using
$\sum_{x\in\FF_q^2}\omega(x,v)=0$ gives
$2\omega(v,s)+0+(q^2-2)\omega(v,s)=q^{2}\omega(v,s)$, which vanishes in the
residue field. What survives is the count of admissible pairs, giving
$R\equiv-2S$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the cancellation]\label{thm:cancel}
$2p_1\equiv p_2\pmod{\lambda^{\vlam(q)+4}}$; equivalently
$e_1+e_2\equiv0\pmod{\lambda^{\vlam(q)+4}}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:closed},
\[
2(q^2-1)\tr H-\tr(H^2)=q\bigl[\,2(q^2-1)S-R-4Q\,\bigr]
\equiv q\bigl[\,2(q^2-1)S+2S\,\bigr]=q\cdot 2q^2S \pmod{q\lambda^4},
\]
and $\vlam(2q^{2}S)=2\vlam(q)+\vlam(S)\ge4$. Dividing by $(q^2-1)^2$, a
$\lambda$-unit, gives $\vlam(2p_1-p_2)\ge\vlam(q)+4$. Finally
$\vlam(p_1^{2})\ge2(\vlam(q)+2)\ge\vlam(q)+4$, so
$e_2=(p_1^{2}-p_2)/2$ yields $e_1+e_2\equiv p_1-p_2/2\equiv0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the sharp law]\label{thm:sharp}
Suppose $\vlam(e_q)\ge\vlam(q)+4$. Then for every odd prime power $q=p^f$,
\[
\det B_t(c)\;\equiv\;\det F\pmod{\lambda^{\,\vlam(q)+4}} .
\]
The hypothesis is automatic whenever $f\ge2$, so for every odd prime power that
is not prime the conclusion is unconditional.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof that the hypothesis is automatic for $f\ge2$]
Every entry of $D$ is a $\ZZ[\zeta_p]$-combination of the $d_v$, each of
valuation $\ge1$, so $D=\lambda D'$ with $D'$ integral and
$\vlam(\det D)\ge q$. As $\vlam(\det F)=0$ we get $\vlam(e_q)\ge q$, and
$q\ge \vlam(q)+4=f(p-1)+4$ holds for every $f\ge2$ (at $q=9$: $9\ge8$; at
$q=27$: $27\ge10$; at $q=25$: $25\ge12$; the margin only grows). For $f=1$ the
inequality reads $q\ge q+3$ and fails, which is why the primes retain a
residual.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
Newton's identities and Lemmas~\ref{lem:power},~\ref{lem:parity} give
$\vlam(e_k)\ge\vlam(q)+4$ for $3\le k\le q-1$: the terms $e_{k-1}p_1$ and
$e_{k-2}p_2$ are products of two quantities of valuation $\ge\vlam(q)+2$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:cong}, and $p_i$ for $i\ge3$ has valuation $\ge\vlam(q)+4$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:cancel} handles $k=1,2$ jointly and the hypothesis handles
$k=q$.
\end{proof}

So the only case still carrying a hypothesis is $q$ prime, where it asks for
$\vlam(\det D)\ge q+3$. Lemma~\ref{lem:top} gives $\vlam(e_q)\ge\vlam(q)+2$
and Newton's identities give $\vlam(\det D)\ge q+1$, both short by two. The
measured value is much larger:
\[
\min_c \vlam(\det D)=2q \qquad (6,\,10,\,14 \text{ at } q=3,5,7),
\]
with equality generically, so the Newton polygon of $D'=D/\lambda$ is the
straight line of slope $-1$ and every eigenvalue of $D$ has valuation exactly
$2$. Since $2q\ge q+3$ for every odd prime, the single statement

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:top}
Every eigenvalue of $D$ has $\lambda$-valuation at least $2$; equivalently
$\vlam(\det D)\ge 2q$.
\end{conjecture}

\noindent closes the prime case and with it the whole law. In terms of the
elementary symmetric functions $e_k=e_k(D)$ the conjecture says exactly
$\vlam(e_k)\ge2k$ for all $k$, and all but the last case is a theorem.

Moreover the last case reduces to a single power-sum bound. Newton's chain
gives $\vlam(e_q)\ge\vlam(p_q)-\vlam(q)=\vlam(p_q)-(q-1)$, so
$\vlam(p_q)\ge2q+2$ would deliver $\vlam(e_q)\ge q+3$ --- exactly what the
sharp law needs. The counting-plus-parity argument only gives $\vlam(p_q)\ge2q$, but that bound
is \emph{not tight}, and the excess has an exact form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factorial}
\vlam\bigl(\tr D^m\bigr)\;=\;\vlam(q)+m+[m\text{ odd}]\;+\;\vlam(m!) .
\end{equation}

\begin{erratum}\label{err:factorial}
\eqref{eq:factorial} is \emph{false at $q=3$}. At $q=3$ there are
$(q^2-1)/2=4$ inverse-closed pairs with $q=3$ choices each, so the section
space has exactly $81$ elements and the minimum can be computed by exhaustion.
Doing so gives
\[
\vlam(\tr D^m)=4,8,8,\mathbf{12},12,\mathbf{16},\mathbf{16},20,20,\mathbf{24},24
\qquad (m=2,\dots,12),
\]
whereas \eqref{eq:factorial} predicts $4,8,8,10,12,14,14,20,20,22,24$: the true
minimum exceeds the stated value by $2$ at $m=5,7,8,11$. All eleven exhaustive
values fit
\[
\vlam(\tr D^m)=2\bigl(m+[m\text{ odd}]\bigr)\qquad (q=3).
\]
Since $2v_3(m!)=m-s_3(m)$ with $s_3$ the base-$3$ digit sum, the two
expressions differ by $s_3(m)+[m\text{ odd}]-2$, which vanishes exactly when
$s_3(m)+[m\text{ odd}]=2$ --- a set containing every $m=3^{j}$. The
prime-power tower is therefore precisely the locus on which the two agree,
which is why its four confirmed rungs $8,20,56,164$ could not detect this.

The scope of the failure is open, but the evidence has moved. At $q=5$ the law
is \emph{not attained} at $m=10$ or $m=14$: the sampled minimum sits exactly
$2$ above it --- the same gap as at $q=3$ --- over $250$ sections, and over
$600$ in a separate probe, while $m=6,8,12$ attain it. Sampling cannot refute,
since the true minimum is at most what a sample exhibits and $q=5$ has $5^{12}$
sections; so $q=5$ remains undecided. But the reading that $q=3$ might be
special --- its section space being the smallest possible --- is now the less
likely of the two, and we withdraw it.

The governing asymmetry is worth stating plainly: \emph{confirming}
\eqref{eq:factorial} at one $(q,m)$ requires only a single attaining section
together with the never-below check, which is cheap; \emph{refuting} it
requires the entire section space, which is available only at $q=3$. Every
confirmation recorded in this paper is of the cheap kind.

For even $m$ the true $q=3$ value is not merely fitted but derived. Across the
complete $81$-section space the pair $(\vlam(e_2),\vlam(e_3))$ takes exactly
four non-degenerate values, and each one determines the whole sequence
$\vlam(\tr D^m)$; the profile is a complete invariant for this data.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:q3even}
Let $q=3$ and let $c$ be one of the $32$ sections with $\det D=0$ and
$\vlam(e_2)=4$. Then $\tr(D^m)=0$ for every odd $m$, and
$\vlam(\tr D^m)=2m$ for every even $m$. In particular the exhaustive even-$m$
minimum is $2m$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Pass 473 $e_1=\tr D=0$, and $e_3=\det D=0$ by hypothesis, so the
characteristic polynomial is $x^3+e_2x=x(x^2+e_2)$. Its roots are $0$ and
$\pm\mu$ with $\mu^2=-e_2$, whence $\vlam(\mu)=\vlam(e_2)/2=2$. Then
$\tr(D^m)=\mu^m+(-\mu)^m$, which vanishes for odd $m$ and equals $2\mu^m$ for
even $m$; as $2$ is a unit at $p=3$, $\vlam(\tr D^m)=m\,\vlam(\mu)=2m$.
\end{proof}

\noindent The parity term is the same fact seen from the other side. These
minimising sections do not cancel by one extra order at odd $m$ --- they vanish
\emph{identically}. Odd $m$ is therefore served by the other profiles, whose
minimum is $2(m+1)$, attained by $(6,6)$ at $m=3,9$ and by $(4,8)$ at $m=5,7$.
So ``$[m\text{ odd}]$'' is not a correction applied to one formula; it is the
switch between which profile attains the minimum. Why those profiles give
$2(m+1)$ is open. Statements below that invoke \eqref{eq:factorial} should be read as
conditional on it, and the reduction of \S\ref{sec:transfer} is unaffected: the
transfer-matrix identity and $T\equiv0\pmod\lambda$ are exact regardless.
\end{erratum}

\noindent The leading term is $\vlam(q)$, not $q-1$; those agree only when $q$ is prime,
and writing $q-1$ makes \eqref{eq:factorial} fail by a constant $20$ already at
$|R|=27$, where $\vlam(27)=6$.
The first three terms are the parity bound; the excess over it is precisely
$\vlam(m!)$. This was found by separating two readings that prime $q$ cannot
distinguish. Over $\FF_9$ and $\FF_3[x]/(x^2)$ --- both of block size $9$ and
characteristic $3$, and giving \emph{identical} profiles --- the excess is not
a single jump at $m=|R|$; it steps at $m=3,6,9$, at multiples of the
\emph{characteristic}, with values $2,2,2,4,4,4,8$ for $m=3,\dots,9$, matching
$\vlam(m!)$ in every case. For prime $q$ the only multiple of $p=q$ in range is
$m=q$ itself, which is why a single jump at the end is all one sees there; and
since $\vlam(q!)=q-1$ for prime $q$, \eqref{eq:factorial} collapses to
$\vlam(\tr D^q)=3q-1$, reproducing $8,14,20,32$ at $q=3,5,7,11$.

Since $3q-1\ge2q+2$ for every $q\ge3$, this settles the residual numerically.

We do \emph{not} claim to know why \eqref{eq:factorial} holds. One tempting
story --- that the $m!$ is what Newton's identities divide by --- is wrong:
Newton computes the $e_k$ \emph{from} the $p_m$, whereas the $p_m=\tr D^m$ are
primary traces computed with no division at all, so no factorial can enter that
way. A mechanism with the right shape is the classical congruence
$\tr(M^{p^k})\equiv\tr(M^{p^{k-1}})\pmod{p^k}$ for integral $M$, which with
$\tr D=0$ forces $\tr(D^{p^k})\equiv0 \pmod{p^k}$ and accumulates exactly as
Legendre's $v_p(m!)=\sum_i\lfloor m/p^i\rfloor$; but we have verified only its
first step, and do not assert it accounts for the whole profile. One ingredient
\emph{is} now proved --- the $[m\text{ odd}]$ bracket, in
\S\ref{sec:oddclass} below.

Two further facts sharpen this. First, \eqref{eq:factorial} \emph{implies} the
determinant law. At $m=q$ it reads $\vlam(\tr D^q)=2q+\vlam(q!)$; Newton's
chain then gives $\vlam(e_q)\ge q+1+\vlam(q!)$, and the law needs
$\vlam(e_q)\ge q+3$, so the entire residual is the inequality
\[
\vlam(q!)\;\ge\;2 ,
\]
which for prime $q$ is $q-1\ge2$. \textbf{The open problem is therefore no
longer a statement about symplectic character sums at the top exponent; it is
\eqref{eq:factorial} itself}, an identity about the whole Newton recursion.
Second, \eqref{eq:factorial} has been tested at $|R|=27$ over both $\FF_{27}$
and $\FF_3[x]/(x^3)$, matching \emph{exactly} at all $26$ exponents and across
all nine increments of $\vlam(m!)$ --- including the double steps at $m=9,18$
and the triple at $m=27$ predicted by Legendre's formula.

\subsection{One ingredient, proved: the odd-class vanishing}
\label{sec:oddclass}

Expanding $\tr(D^m)$ over $m$-tuples $(v_1,\dots,v_m)$ of total displacement
zero, the summand is invariant under cyclic rotation, so the trace decomposes
over the cyclic orbits,
\[
\tr(D^m)\;=\;\sum_{d\mid m} d\,S_d ,\qquad
S_d=\sum_{\text{orbits of minimal period }d}\ \text{value at a representative}.
\]
The following says that at odd $m$ most of these classes are not merely small
but identically zero.

\begin{theorem}[odd-class vanishing]\label{thm:oddclass}
Write $m=dk$. If $m$ is odd and $p\mid k$, then $S_d=0$ identically, for every
inverse-closed section.
\end{theorem}

\noindent The hypothesis is not really about $p$. Both ingredients below use
$p$ only through the order $e$ of the generating character, which over $\FF_q$
happens to equal $p$: step~(i) needs $kv=0$ for every $v$, and step~(ii) needs
$\operatorname{ord}(u)\mid k$ for every $u$ in the image of $\psi$ --- and each
of those is $e\mid k$. The theorem therefore holds verbatim over any finite
Frobenius ring with generating character of order $e$, with $p\mid k$ replaced
by $e\mid k$; and over $\ZZ/9$, where $e=9$, the constant class is measured to
vanish at $m=9$ and $m=27$ and \emph{not} at $m=3$, exactly where ``$p\mid m$''
would have predicted vanishing. The order of the generating character is the
same invariant that governs the scope of the determinant law itself
(\S\ref{sec:pf}): two statements proved and tested separately are indexed by
the same number.

\begin{proof}
Since $m=dk$ is odd, both $d$ and $k$ are odd. Let $(v_1,\dots,v_d)$ represent
an orbit of minimal period $d$ and put $M=\rho_{v_1}\cdots\rho_{v_d}$. By the
Heisenberg cocycle $M=\zeta^{s}\rho(w)$ with $w=\sum_i v_i$, so
$M^{k}=\zeta^{ks}\rho(kw)$; as $p\mid k$ this kills both the phase and the
argument, giving $M^{k}=I$ and $\tr(M^{k})=q\in\ZZ$.

The coefficient of the orbit is $\prod_{i\le d}(u_i-1)^{k}$ with each $u_i$ a
$p$-th root of unity. For $u=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j/p}$ one has
$u-1=e^{\mathrm{i}\pi j/p}\,2\mathrm{i}\sin(\pi j/p)$, hence
\[
(u-1)^{k}=e^{\mathrm{i}k\pi j/p}\,(2\mathrm{i})^{k}\sin^{k}(\pi j/p),
\]
which is purely imaginary exactly when $\tfrac{k-1}{2}+\tfrac{kj}{p}\in\ZZ$ for
every $j$, i.e.\ exactly when $k$ is odd and $p\mid k$ --- our hypothesis. A
product of $d$ purely imaginary numbers is purely imaginary because $d$ is odd.

Finally, inverse closure gives $c(-v)=-c(v)$, hence $d_{-v}=\overline{d_v}$, so
the orbit of $(v_1,\dots,v_m)$ pairs with that of $(-v_1,\dots,-v_m)$ and the
two together contribute $q\cdot 2\operatorname{Re}(\text{purely imaginary})=0$.
Summing over pairs gives $S_d=0$.
\end{proof}

For $d=1$ the hypothesis is also necessary, and the witness is explicit rather
than found by search.

\begin{proposition}[the converse at $d=1$]\label{prop:converse1}
Let $p\mid m$, so that constant orbits exist. Then $S_1=0$ for every
inverse-closed section if and only if $m$ is odd.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Sufficiency is Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass}. For necessity, fix one
inverse-closed pair $\{v_0,-v_0\}$ and take the section with
$\psi(c(v_0))=u\neq1$, $c(-v_0)=-c(v_0)$, and $c(v)=0$ for every other $v$;
this is inverse-closed by construction. Every $d_v$ vanishes except
$d_{v_0}=u-1$ and $d_{-v_0}=\bar u-1$, so
\[
S_1 \;=\; q\bigl[(u-1)^{m}+\overline{(u-1)^{m}}\bigr]
\;=\; 2q\operatorname{Re}(u-1)^{m},
\]
which is nonzero exactly when $(u-1)^m$ is not purely imaginary --- by the
criterion in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass}, exactly when $m$ is even.
\end{proof}

\noindent For $d>1$ the converse is verified on $28$ cells and remains
unproved.

Two remarks. First, $p\nmid m/d$ precisely when $d$ carries the whole $p$-part
of $m$, so at odd $m$ the decomposition \emph{collapses}: the surviving classes
are indexed by the divisors of $m/p^{\,v_p(m)}$, and when $m$ is a power of $p$
exactly one class survives, the free class $d=m$. Numerically the free class
then carries the entire valuation --- verified at $m=p$ and at $m=p^{2}=9$,
where both short classes die in all twelve sampled sections. So the clean
behaviour at $m=p$ is not a small-case accident; it is the prime-power case.

This makes $m=p^{j}$ the one place where \eqref{eq:factorial} can be read off
with no inter-class cancellation to confound it, and there the Legendre tower
is exact:
\[
\begin{array}{llll}
p=3: & \vlam(\tr D^{3})=8, & \vlam(\tr D^{9})=20, & \vlam(\tr D^{27})=56;\\
p=5: & \vlam(\tr D^{5})=14, & \vlam(\tr D^{25})=54, &
\end{array}
\]
each equal to $\vlam(q)+m+1+\vlam(m!)$ --- minima over $250$ and $60$ sampled
sections respectively --- and including the double increment at $m=25$ and the
\emph{triple} increment at $m=27$, where $v_3(27!)=9+3+1=13$.

A single surviving class also means $\tr(D^m)=m\,S_m$ exactly, so what the
orbit \emph{weight} contributes separates from what the orbit \emph{sum}
contributes, with no enumeration: $\vlam(S_m)=\vlam(\tr D^m)-\vlam(m)$, giving
$6,16,50$ at $p=3$ and $10,46$ at $p=5$. The weight supplies $\vlam(m)=j(p-1)$,
that is $j$ of the $\sum_i\lfloor m/p^i\rfloor$ Legendre terms --- all of them
at $j=1$, half at $j=2$, and $3/13$ at $j=3$. So the orbit mechanism accounts
for the first Legendre increment and a vanishing share thereafter. That is a
quantified limit on the mechanism, not an account of the remainder.

Second, the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass} is the bracket
$[m\text{ odd}]$ of \eqref{eq:factorial}. That term --- the one ingredient of
the law that had the look of a fitted correction --- is exactly the condition
under which these classes vanish, and Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass} is the first
ingredient of \eqref{eq:factorial} with a proof rather than a measurement. It
uses inverse closure, the same hypothesis that rescues the first power sum
(Lemma~\ref{lem:parity}) and the top exterior power.

Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass} says nothing when $m$ is even. Both it and the even
case are consequences of one statement.

\begin{theorem}[the sieve]\label{thm:sieve}
Let $e$ be the order of the generating character and let $t\mid m$ satisfy:
$m/t$ is odd and $e\mid(m/t)$. Then
\[
\sum_{d\mid t} d\,S_d \;=\; q\Bigl(\sum_{v\neq0}d_v^{\,m/t}\Bigr)^{t}\;=\;0 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $n=m/t$. For $d\mid t$ we have $n\mid(m/d)$, hence $e\mid(m/d)$, so a
period-$d$ representative $(w_1,\dots,w_d)$ has
$M^{m/d}=\zeta^{s\,m/d}\rho\bigl((m/d)w\bigr)=I$ and the zero-sum condition
$(m/d)\sum_i w_i=0$ is automatic. So $\tr(M^{m/d})=q$ and the orbit's value is
$q\prod_{i\le d}d_{w_i}^{\,m/d}$, which is $\prod_{i\le t}d_{w_i}^{\,n}$ read
over the repeated $t$-tuple. Since $d\,S_d$ counts each period-$d$ orbit once
per distinct $t$-tuple in it, summing over $d\mid t$ sweeps every $t$-tuple
exactly once and gives $q\bigl(\sum_v d_v^{\,n}\bigr)^{t}$. The bracket
vanishes because $n$ is odd and $e\mid n$: $(u-1)^n$ is purely imaginary and
inverse closure pairs $v$ with $-v$.
\end{proof}

Write $T=\{\,t\mid m:\ m/t\ \text{odd},\ e\mid(m/t)\,\}$. For $m$ \emph{odd},
$T$ is exactly the set of divisors of $m/e$ --- downward closed --- so
M\"obius inversion runs: $t=1$ gives $S_1=0$, and then
$t\,S_t=-\sum_{d\mid t,\,d<t}d\,S_d=0$ for every $t\mid(m/e)$. That is
precisely the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass}, so the odd-class
vanishing theorem is a corollary. For $m$ \emph{even}, $T$ is not downward
closed --- at $m=jp$ only $t=j$ survives --- and Theorem~\ref{thm:sieve}
degenerates to the single relation $\sum_{d\mid j}d\,S_d=0$. One theorem covers
both, and the difference between the odd and even cases becomes the question of
whether a divisor set is downward closed.

At $m=2p$ the relation reads $S_1+2S_2=0$ identically --- verified exactly at
$(p,m)=(3,6)$, $(5,10)$, $(7,14)$ --- so the residual there lives entirely in
the class $d=p$, which $t=2$ does not reach. This corrects a reading in an
earlier draft, which lumped $d=3$ together with $d=1,2$ at $(3,6)$ and concluded
that the short aggregate generically sits below the total; the aggregate over
$d\mid t$ is not merely small but zero.

When $\lvert T\rvert>1$ the theorem gives constraints neither of its two
corollaries reaches. At $m=18$ with $e=3$ one has $T=\{2,6\}$, and subtracting
the two relations leaves
\[
3S_3+6S_6=0 ,
\]
a relation at \emph{even} $m$ that is not an individual vanishing: neither
$S_3$ nor $S_6$ is zero on its own in any section sampled.

\subsection{How much the sieve pins}

Since Pass 510 the standing verdict has been that the orbit account is
\emph{partial}. Theorem~\ref{thm:sieve} makes that a dimension count.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rank}
The sieve system on the unknowns $\{S_d:d\mid m\}$ has rank $\lvert T\rvert$.
Hence, with $U=\{u\mid m:\ u \text{ odd},\ e\mid u\}$,
\[
\mathrm{pinned}(m)=\lvert U\rvert,\qquad
\mathrm{free}(m)=\tau(m)-\lvert U\rvert ,
\]
and writing $m=2^{b}s$ with $s$ odd,
$\mathrm{free}(m)=(b+1)\tau(s)-\tau(s/e)$ when $e\mid s$, and $\tau(m)$
otherwise.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Relation $t$ involves exactly the $S_d$ with $d\mid t$, and $S_t$ occurs in
relation $t$ and in no relation indexed by a proper divisor of $t$. So along
any linear extension of divisibility the system is triangular with nonzero
diagonal entries $t$, and its rank is the number of relations, $\lvert
T\rvert=\lvert U\rvert$. The closed form is the divisor count
$\lvert U\rvert=\tau(s/e)$ when $e\mid s$ and $0$ otherwise, together with
$\tau(m)=(b+1)\tau(s)$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Checked against the rank of the actual matrix over $\QQ$ for
$e\in\{3,5,7,9,25,27\}$ and $m\le60$.

One may ask whether some further symmetry of the summand supplies relations the
sieve misses. That is decidable rather than arguable: measure the classes
themselves across many sections, expand each into its coordinates, and take the
rank. The nullity is then the dimension of the space of universally valid
linear relations with constant coefficients.

\begin{erratum}\label{err:complete}
An earlier draft reported that this nullity equals $\lvert T\rvert$ at
$(p,m)=(3,3),(3,6),(3,9),(3,15),(5,5),(7,7)$ and concluded that the sieve is
not merely a family of relations but all of them. \emph{The sieve is not
complete.} Choosing cells where $\lvert T\rvert=0$, so that the sieve predicts
no relations at all, the measured nullity is $1$ --- at $(3,2)$, $(3,4)$,
$(3,10)$, $(5,4)$ and $(5,6)$ alike. The missing relation is elementary: the
period-one class consists of the constant $m$-tuples $(v,\dots,v)$, whose
zero-sum condition is $mv=0$ with $v\neq0$, solvable exactly when $p\mid m$; so
for $p\nmid m$ the class is \emph{empty} and $S_1=0$ holds vacuously. The sieve
counts relations forced by cancellation and does not count vacuous classes. The
corrected statement is
\[
\text{nullity}\;=\;\lvert T\rvert+[\,p\nmid m\,],
\]
verified on eleven cells across both regimes.

Every cell used in the earlier draft satisfies $p\mid m$, so the correction term
vanished in all of them. The cells were not chosen adversarially: the shortcut
of \S\ref{sec:closedform} requires $e\mid(m/d)$, a condition on $p$ dividing
$m$, so the affordable cells were exactly the cells where the claim cannot
fail. Convenience and the blind spot were the same constraint --- the second
such instance in this paper, after the prime-power tower and
\eqref{eq:factorial}.
\end{erratum}

\noindent The scope of what remains is worth stating plainly: relations whose
coefficients depend on the section, and nonlinear relations, are not excluded,
and the corrected law is itself a measurement over sampled sections rather than
a proof. A larger sample can only lower a measured rank and hence raise the
nullity, so an excess relation found this way cannot be a sampling artefact in
the direction that matters.

At a prime power $m=p^{j}$ the count is
$\mathrm{free}=1$: the sieve pins $j$ of the $j+1$ classes and leaves only the
free class $d=m$. So the whole Legendre tower above the first increment sits in
a single class, in which no cancellation between classes can occur; the orbit
weight supplies $j$ of the $\sum_i\lfloor m/p^i\rfloor$ Legendre terms and the
orbit sum must supply the remaining $\sum_i\lfloor m/p^i\rfloor-j$ of them ---
that is $0,2,10,36$ terms at $p=3$ for $j=1,2,3,4$, or equivalently
$(p-1)$ times that, $0,4,20,72$ orders of $\lambda$. That is the sharpest form
we have of what remains unproved. The tower itself is exact at every rung
measured, as minima over sampled sections:
\[
\begin{array}{lllll}
p=3: & 8, & 20, & 56, & 164 \quad (m=3,9,27,81);\\
p=5: & 14, & 54 & & \quad (m=5,25);\\
p=7: & 20, & 104 & & \quad (m=7,49).
\end{array}
\]

The sieve also survives where the determinant law does not. Its proof uses only
$e\mid(m/d)$, which holds over $\ZZ/p^{n}$ exactly as over a field, and nothing
in it uses \eqref{eq:factorial}; the relations are verified to hold at
$\ZZ/9$, $\ZZ/25$ and $\ZZ/27$. That separates the structural facts that
survive the failure from the arithmetic one that does not.

It is tempting to go further and read the failure depths $12,30,36$ as a shadow
of how much the sieve leaves free. \emph{They are not.} At the relevant
exponent $m=q$ the free counts are $2,2,3$ at $\ZZ/9,\ZZ/25,\ZZ/27$, so
$\ZZ/9$ and $\ZZ/25$ share a free count while their depths differ by $18$; no
function of the free count alone can produce both. We record the candidate as
refused. The depths still match $p^{n-1}(p+1)$ exactly and remain unexplained.

\subsection{A closed form, and the sieve in two lines}
\label{sec:closedform}

The classes need not be enumerated at all. Write
$\mathrm{Ps}(k)=\sum_{v\neq0}d_v^{\,k}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:closed}
Whenever $e\mid(m/d)$,
\[
d\,S_d \;=\; q\sum_{c\mid d}\mu(d/c)\,\mathrm{Ps}(m/c)^{\,c}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $x_v=d_v^{\,m/d}$. By the shortcut above, $d\,S_d$ is $q$ times the sum of
$\prod_i x_{w_i}$ over $d$-tuples of exact period $d$. A $d$-tuple has period
dividing $c\mid d$ exactly when it is a $c$-tuple repeated $d/c$ times, and
then its product is $\bigl(\prod_{i\le c}x_{w_i}\bigr)^{d/c}$; summing over all
$c$-tuples gives $\bigl(\sum_v x_v^{\,d/c}\bigr)^{c}=\mathrm{Ps}(m/c)^{c}$.
M\"obius inversion over the divisor lattice converts ``period dividing'' into
``period exactly''.
\end{proof}

\noindent This gives Theorem~\ref{thm:sieve} again, in two lines: summing over
$d\mid t$ and exchanging the order of summation,
\[
\sum_{d\mid t}d\,S_d
=q\sum_{c\mid t}\mathrm{Ps}(m/c)^{c}\!\!\sum_{c\mid d\mid t}\!\!\mu(d/c)
=q\,\mathrm{Ps}(m/t)^{t},
\]
the inner sum being $1$ at $c=t$ and $0$ otherwise. The counting proof given
above and this one are both correct; this one says where the theorem comes
from.

Computationally the difference is decisive. A class now costs $\tau(d)$ power
sums, each a single loop over the $q^2-1$ vectors, in place of $\lvert
V\rvert^{d}$ tuples --- so $(7,49)$ at $d=7$, which would have needed $48^{7}$
tuples, and $(3,27)$ at $d=9$, which would have needed $8^{9}$, become
immediate.

The hypothesis $e\mid(m/d)$ is not an artefact, and what it excludes can be
named exactly. The representation satisfies
\[
\rho(v)\,\rho(w)=\zeta^{-\omega(v,w)}\rho(v+w),\qquad
\omega\bigl((a,b),(a',b')\bigr)=ab'-a'b,
\]
so a period-$d$ representative gives $M=\rho_{w_1}\cdots\rho_{w_d}
=\zeta^{-\Omega}\rho(w)$ with $w=\sum_i w_i$ and
$\Omega=\sum_{i<j}\omega(w_i,w_j)$; and since $\omega(w,w)=0$ one has
$\rho(w)^{k}=\rho(kw)$ exactly. With $k=m/d$ the zero-sum condition on the
$m$-tuple is precisely $kw=0$, so $\tr(M^{k})=q\,\zeta^{-k\Omega}$ and the
orbit's value is
\[
q\,\zeta^{-k\Omega}\prod_{i\le d}d_{w_i}^{\,k}.
\]
The shortcut above is this formula with its phase dropped, and the phase is
trivial for \emph{every} orbit exactly when $e\mid k$. That is why the closed
form stops at the top class, where $k=1$: not a gap in the argument but the
shape of the object.

\subsection{The transfer matrix}\label{sec:transfer}

The phase does more than obstruct: it organises. Writing $P_j=v_1+\dots+v_j$
for the partial sums, the exponent telescopes,
\[
\Omega=\sum_{i<j}\omega(v_i,v_j)=\sum_j \omega(P_{j-1},v_j),
\]
because $\sum_{i<j}\omega(v_i,v_j)=\sum_j\omega\bigl(\sum_{i<j}v_i,\,v_j\bigr)$.
So the weight of an $m$-tuple factorises along the walk it traces through
$R^{2}$, and the whole trace becomes a single matrix entry.

\begin{theorem}[transfer matrix]\label{thm:transfer}
Let $T$ be the $q^{2}\times q^{2}$ matrix on partial-sum states given by
\[
T[P+v,\;P]=d_v\,\zeta^{-\omega(P,v)}\qquad (v\neq0).
\]
Then $\tr(D^m)=q\,[T^m]_{0,0}$ for every $m$ and every section.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\rho(v_1)\cdots\rho(v_m)=\zeta^{-\Omega}\rho\bigl(\sum_i v_i\bigr)$ by the
cocycle, and $\tr\rho(x)=q$ for $x=0$ and $0$ otherwise. So $\tr(D^m)$ is $q$
times the sum, over $m$-tuples with $\sum_i v_i=0$, of
$\zeta^{-\Omega}\prod_i d_{v_i}$. By the telescoping identity that summand is
$\prod_j d_{v_j}\zeta^{-\omega(P_{j-1},v_j)}$, the product of the edge weights
along the walk $0=P_0,P_1,\dots,P_m=0$; summing over closed walks of length $m$
based at $0$ is $[T^m]_{0,0}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[translation covariance]\label{thm:cov}
For $a\in R^2$ let $S_a$ permute the states by $P\mapsto P+a$ and let $U_a$ be
diagonal with entries $\zeta^{\omega(a,P)}$. Then $U_aT=S_aTS_a^{-1}U_a$ for
every $a$. Consequently $[T^m]_{P,P}=[T^m]_{0,0}$ for every $P$, and
$\tr(T^m)=q\,\tr(D^m)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Both sides send the basis vector at $P$ to a multiple of the one at $P+v$; the
multipliers are $d_v\zeta^{\omega(a,P+v)-\omega(P,v)}$ and
$d_v\zeta^{-\omega(P-a,v)+\omega(a,P)}$, equal since $\omega$ is bilinear and
alternating. Conjugation by $S_aU_a$ therefore carries the $(0,0)$ entry of
$T^m$ to the $(a,a)$ entry, so the diagonal is constant; summing it gives
$\tr(T^m)=q^2[T^m]_{0,0}=q\,\tr(D^m)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer}.
\end{proof}

\noindent This explains the factor $q$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer}: it is not
a normalisation accident but the count of states in a translation orbit. It
also makes the open residue $E(m)=\vlam([T^m]_{0,0})-m$ a statement about
$\tr(T^m)$, hence a \emph{similarity invariant} of $T$ --- something the cyclic
decomposition, whose classes are not similarity invariants, could never see.

\noindent Everything in this section is a statement about closed walks in this
weighted graph, grouped by rotation of the base point: the period-$d$ classes
are cycle types, Theorem~\ref{thm:sieve} is a M\"obius inversion over them, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:closed} is the necklace count.

The reformulation removes one summand of \eqref{eq:factorial} outright. Every
entry of $T$ is a $\ZZ[\zeta]$-combination of the $d_v$, and $\vlam(d_v)\ge1$
for every $v$; hence $T\equiv0\pmod\lambda$ and
\[
\vlam\bigl([T^m]_{0,0}\bigr)\;\ge\;m
\]
with no work at all. Since $\tr(D^m)=q[T^m]_{0,0}$ contributes the $\vlam(q)$,
what remains of \eqref{eq:factorial} is exactly the excess
\[
E(m):=\vlam\bigl([T^m]_{0,0}\bigr)-m=[m\text{ odd}]+\vlam(m!) ,
\]
a statement about one entry of one matrix. We do not prove it; we record that
this is now all that is left to prove.

A corollary of the sieve is worth recording in this language. Taking $t=m/e$
gives $\sum_{d\mid m/e}d\,S_d=0$, so
\[
\tr(D^m)=\sum_{\substack{d\mid m\\ d\nmid m/e}} d\,S_d :
\]
the trace is carried entirely by the classes whose period does \emph{not}
divide $m/e$. At $m=p^{j}$ the only such class is $d=m$, recovering
$\tr(D^m)=m\,S_m$; at $(p,m)=(3,6)$ the carriers are $d=3$ and $d=6$.

Proposition~\ref{prop:closed} also settles which $m$ have a single free class.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:freeone}
$\mathrm{free}(m)=1$ if and only if $m$ is a power of $p$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $m$ odd write $m=p^{a}r$ with $\gcd(r,p)=1$; then
$\lvert U\rvert=\tau(m/e)=a\,\tau(r)$ and $\tau(m)=(a+1)\tau(r)$, so
$\mathrm{free}=\tau(r)$, which is $1$ exactly when $r=1$. For $m$ even write
$m=2^{b}s$ with $b\ge1$ and $s$ odd; then
$\mathrm{free}=(b+1)\tau(s)-\tau(s/e)\ge2\tau(s)-\tau(s/e)>\tau(s)\ge1$, since
$\tau(s/e)<\tau(s)$ whenever $e>1$ divides $s$. So no even $m$ qualifies.
\end{proof}

\begin{erratum}\label{err:freeone}
Proposition~\ref{prop:freeone} is \emph{false}. Its proof is correct given its
input, but that input --- $\mathrm{free}(m)=\tau(m)-\lvert U\rvert$ --- omits
the vacuous class of Erratum~\ref{err:complete}. With the corrected count
$\mathrm{free}(m)=\tau(m)-\lvert T\rvert-[\,p\nmid m\,]$, any prime
$\ell\neq p$ has $\tau=2$, $\lvert T\rvert=0$ and correction $1$, hence
$\mathrm{free}=1$. Measured at $(p,m)=(3,5),(3,7),(5,3),(5,7),(7,3)$:
$\mathrm{free}=1$ in every case and none is a power of $p$. The corrected
statement is that $\mathrm{free}(m)=1$ for $m$ a power of $p$ \emph{and} for
$m$ a prime other than $p$.

A proof inherits the blind spot of the formula it starts from. This is the
third instance in this paper; the failure mode itself, with all three examples,
is written up separately in \texttt{papers/agreement\_locus.tex}.
\end{erratum}

\noindent The repaired characterisation is that $\mathrm{free}(m)=1$ exactly
when $m$ is a power of $p$ \emph{or} a prime different from $p$, with
$\mathrm{free}(1)=0$: if $p\mid m$ then $\mathrm{free}=\tau(m)-\lvert
T\rvert$, equal to $1$ only for $m=p^{a}$; if $p\nmid m$ then
$\mathrm{free}=\tau(m)-1$, equal to $1$ exactly when $\tau(m)=2$.

We also record the status of Erratum~\ref{err:complete}'s corrected law more
precisely than ``measured''. Its lower bound
$\text{nullity}\ge\lvert T\rvert+[\,p\nmid m\,]$ is proved: both relation
families are genuine and, since $\lvert T\rvert\ge1$ exactly when $p\mid m$,
they never coexist. Its upper bound comes from exhibiting sections whose class
vectors are linearly independent over $\QQ(\zeta_p)$ --- and exhibiting
independent vectors \emph{proves} a rank lower bound rather than sampling one,
the independence being exact arithmetic. So for each cell where the two bounds
meet, the equality is a theorem about that cell; what remains open is the
statement for all $m$ simultaneously, which no finite computation can settle.

The closed form also lets the completeness test be redone with coefficients in
$\QQ(\zeta_p)$ rather than $\QQ$, by carrying out the elimination in the field
itself --- inverting through the multiplication matrix on the reduced power
basis. The nullity again equals $\lvert T\rvert$ --- every one of these cells has
$p\mid m$, so by Erratum~\ref{err:complete} the correction term
$[\,p\nmid m\,]$ vanishes throughout and the agreement is expected --- now at
$(p,m)=(3,6),(3,9),(3,15),(3,27),(3,81),(5,25),(7,49)$, the last four of which
only the closed form makes computable. One caution the first run supplied: at
odd $m$ a large fraction of sections give $\tr(D^m)=0$ identically, so their
entire class vector vanishes and contributes nothing to the rank. Sampling a
fixed range of sections drew only such sections at $m=15,27,81$ and returned
rank $0$, which looks like a missing relation and is really an empty sample;
the figures above count \emph{informative} sections.

Finally, the proof's first step is a useful computational tool in its own
right: whenever $e\mid(m/d)$, a period-$d$ orbit's value is
$q\prod_i d_{w_i}^{\,m/d}$ with no matrix products at all. Checked against the
honest matrix computation on $15{,}616$ orbits over $\FF_3,\FF_5$ and on the
constant class over $\ZZ/9$, it is what puts $\ZZ/25$ and $\ZZ/27$ --- $624$
and $728$ vectors carrying $25\times25$ and $27\times27$ blocks --- within
reach, and there the character-order form of the hypothesis is confirmed at a
second $p^2$ and a first $p^3$.

The orbit account is therefore partial for a sharper reason than ``the even
case is untouched'': what remains unexplained at $m=jp$ is exactly the classes
with $d\nmid j$. At $(3,6)$ the total ranges over $12,14,18$ by section, with
minimum $12=\vlam(3)+6+0+\vlam(6!)$, the value predicted by
\eqref{eq:factorial} and consistent with the law being a minimum over sections.

Finally, \eqref{eq:factorial} fails --- and fails \emph{from below} --- exactly
where the determinant law fails. Over $\ZZ/9$ and $\ZZ/25$, whose generating
characters have order $p^2$, the measured power sums lie strictly under the
right-hand side (at $\ZZ/9$, $m=9$: $30$ against $46$). So the two failures
share a locus and presumably a cause, and \eqref{eq:factorial} is not a more
robust statement than the law it implies.

Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass} sharpens that observation from a shared locus to a
named broken hypothesis. Ingredient~(i) needs $\rho_v^{\,p}=I$, which came from
$(v,0)^p=(pv,0)$ being the identity in characteristic $p$; over $\ZZ/p^{n}$ it
is not, and the failure is severe --- over $\ZZ/9$ only $8$ of the $80$
nonzero vectors satisfy $\rho_v^{\,3}=I$ (exactly those with $3v=0$), and over
$\ZZ/25$ only $24$ of $624$. Correspondingly the constant class at $m=p$, which
Theorem~\ref{thm:oddclass} kills over $\FF_q$, is measured to be nonzero over
both rings. So in the failure region a specific hypothesis of the proof is
false and the corresponding conclusion is false. We record this as a
\emph{candidate}: it identifies where the argument breaks, and it does not
establish that this is why \eqref{eq:factorial} fails.

\begin{theorem}[the eigenvalue bound, off the top]\label{thm:ek2k}
For every odd prime $q$ and every inverse-closed section,
$\vlam(e_k)\ge 2k$ for all $1\le k\le q-1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $k$. Since $p_1=\tr D=0$, Newton's identity reads
$k\,e_k=\sum_{i=2}^{k}(-1)^{i-1}e_{k-i}p_i$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:power}
$\vlam(p_i)\ge\vlam(q)+i$, and by induction $\vlam(e_{k-i})\ge2(k-i)$, so each
term has valuation at least
\[
2(k-i)+\vlam(q)+i \;=\; 2k-i+\vlam(q)\;\ge\;2k ,
\]
because $i\le k\le q-1=\vlam(q)$. For $k\le q-1$ the integer $k$ is a unit at
$\lambda$, so dividing by it is harmless.
\end{proof}

The induction fails at $k=q$ for one identifiable reason: there $i$ may equal
$q$, and the inequality $i\le\vlam(q)=q-1$ misses by exactly one. Accordingly
Newton yields only $\vlam(\det D)\ge q+1$ against the measured $2q$. So the
entire residual gap in the determinant law is the single coefficient
$e_q=\det D$ of one characteristic polynomial.

Two further pieces of evidence. First, $e_2=qS$ exactly, from
$e_2=(p_1^2-p_2)/2=-p_2/2$ and $p_2=-2qS$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:closed}); this is the
second exact closed form in the family after $\tr H=qS$. Second, $D'$ reduces
mod $\lambda$ to $\sum_a\kappa_aP_a$ with $\kappa_{-a}=-\kappa_a$ (so
$\kappa_0=0$ and $\sum_a\kappa_a=0$), which lies in the augmentation ideal of
$\FF_q[\ZZ/q]=\FF_q[s]/(s^{q})$ --- a nilpotent ideal. Hence every eigenvalue
of $D'$ is a nonunit, giving valuation $>0$; the conjecture upgrades this to
$\ge1$.

\begin{remark}
The exponent is attained: at $q=3$ every non-flat section gives valuation
exactly $6=\vlam(q)+4$, at $q=5$ the observed values are $\{8,10,12\}$ with
minimum $8$, at $q=7$ the minimum is $10$, and at $q=9$ --- where
$\vlam(9)=4$, not $q-1=8$ --- the minimum is $8$. All four agree with
$\vlam(q)+4$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
The residue class is the flat determinant, not a numerical coincidence of small
$q$. At $q=3$ the two determinant values are $-16$ (flat orbit) and $11$, which
differ by $27$; since $(\lambda^{6})=(27)$ for $q=3$, the congruence
$\det B\equiv\det F\pmod{\lambda^{q+3}}$ there reads $\det B\equiv-16\pmod{27}$,
and $11\equiv-16$. An earlier guess that the residue is $q^2+2$ fails already
at $q=5$.
\end{remark}

\section{Prime powers}\label{sec:pf}

Let $q=p^f$ with $f>1$, $\psi_t(z)=\zeta_p^{\operatorname{Tr}_{\FF_q/\FF_p}(tz)}$,
and $\lambda=1-\zeta_p$. Lemma~\ref{lem:flat} survives verbatim: at $q=9$ one
finds $F^2+2F-80I=0$, spectrum $\{8^5,(-10)^4\}$, and
$\det F=8^5(-10)^4=327{,}680{,}000$, in agreement with the formula of
Lemma~\ref{lem:flat}; the trace laws hold as well.

Nothing else changes. Every ingredient of the argument --- the flat-block
Lemma~\ref{lem:flat}, the vanishing of $\tr\rho_t$ off the centre
(Lemma~\ref{lem:trace}), the pairing Lemma~\ref{lem:pair}, the parity
Lemma~\ref{lem:parity}, and the two closed forms of Lemma~\ref{lem:closed} ---
holds verbatim with $\psi_t$ in place of $\zeta^{t\,\cdot}$ and $\omega$ the
symplectic form over $\FF_q$. In particular the symplectic cancellations go
through because $\sum_{x\in\FF_q^2}\omega(x,v)=0$ for $v\neq0$ and because
$q^{2}\equiv0$ in the residue field, neither of which sees $f$.

The single quantity that depends on $f$ is the ramification,
\[
\vlam(q)=\vlam(p^{f})=f(p-1),
\]
since $(p)=(\lambda)^{p-1}$ in $\ZZ[\zeta_p]$. Theorems~\ref{thm:cong},
\ref{thm:cancel} and \ref{thm:sharp} were therefore already stated in their
correct generality: \emph{there is no separate prime-power theory}.

\begin{remark}[why this is one law and not two]
At prime $q$ we have $\vlam(q)=q-1$, so the sharp exponent
$\vlam(q)+4$ reads $q+3$; at $q=9$ we have $\vlam(9)=4$ and it reads $8$. As
functions of $q$ these look unrelated --- $8$ sits far below the value
$q+3=12$ that the prime formula would predict --- which is why we first
recorded the two cases as different phenomena, the prime case as a law and the
prime-power case as its failure. As functions of $\vlam(q)$ they are one
statement. The constant $4=2+2$ counts inverse closure twice, once at first
order and once in the $e_1/e_2$ cancellation, and is blind to the
factorization of $q$; only the ramification varies.
\end{remark}

The measured minima agree with $\vlam(q)+4$ at every prime power tested,
across three different values of $f$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccccc@{}}
\toprule
$q$ & $3$ & $5$ & $7$ & $9$ & $25$ & $49$ & $27$ & $81$\\
$(p,f)$ & $(3,1)$ & $(5,1)$ & $(7,1)$ & $(3,2)$ & $(5,2)$ & $(7,2)$ & $(3,3)$ & $(3,4)$\\
\midrule
$\vlam(q)$ & $2$ & $4$ & $6$ & $4$ & $8$ & $12$ & $6$ & $8$\\
$\vlam(q)+4$ & $6$ & $8$ & $10$ & $8$ & $12$ & $16$ & $10$ & $12$\\
observed min & $6$ & $8$ & $10$ & $8$ & $12$ & $16$ & $10$ & $12$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Eight prime powers across four values of $f$; the last five columns are
unconditional by Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp}. Note that $q$ and $\vlam(q)$ order
the columns differently --- $q=49$ has a larger exponent than $q=81$ --- which
is the clearest sign that the ramification, not $q$, is the governing
parameter. The determinants beyond $q=9$ were computed by fraction-free
(Bareiss) elimination over $\ZZ[\zeta_p]$, validated against cofactor
expansion at $q=3,5,7$; the flat determinants match Lemma~\ref{lem:flat}
exactly in every case, including the $35$- and $39$-digit values
$24^{13}26^{12}$ at $q=25$ and $-26^{14}28^{13}$ at $q=27$.

\subsection{The law does not survive over $\ZZ/p^{n}$}

It is natural to ask whether the field can be replaced by a finite chain ring.
It cannot, and the failure is instructive. Take $q=9$ but with the ring
$\ZZ/9$ in place of $\FF_9$: the group, the sections and the inverse-closure
condition are unchanged, and only the central character changes, from order
$3$ to order $9$. The construction remains valid --- $\rho$ is a homomorphism,
$\tr\rho$ vanishes off the centre, and the flat block still satisfies
$F^2+2F-80I=0$ with spectrum $\{8^5,(-10)^4\}$ and $\det F=327{,}680{,}000$,
numerically identical to the field case. But now $\lambda=1-\zeta_9$ and
$\vlam(9)=12$, so the law would predict exponent $16$.

The measured exponent is $12=\vlam(q)$: over $20$ sections the depths are
$\{12,16,18\}$. \emph{The entire $+4$ is lost.} The mechanism is exact, and it
is a single one. Newton's identity divides by $k$, which is harmless only when
$k$ is a $\lambda$-unit; and while $\vlam(3)=2$ in $\ZZ[\zeta_3]$, in
$\ZZ[\zeta_9]$ one has $\vlam(3)=6$. With block size $9$ the recursion passes
through $k=3,6,9$ in both cases, and only when the character has order $9$ is
the loss large enough to consume the whole $+4$.

It is tempting to blame the ring structure --- $\ZZ/9$ has zero divisors and
nonzero vectors that are not unimodular, so one might expect the symplectic
sums of Lemma~\ref{lem:closed} to degrade. \emph{They do not.} For any finite
Frobenius ring $R$ with generating character $\psi$ one has
$\sum_{a\in R}\psi(au)=0$ for every $u\neq0$ --- that is the defining property
--- so
\[
\sum_{x\in R^2}\psi\bigl(\omega(x,u)\bigr)
=\Bigl(\sum_{x_0}\psi(x_0u_1)\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{x_1}\psi(-u_0x_1)\Bigr)=0
\]
for every $u\neq0$, unimodular or not; we verified this exhaustively over
$\ZZ/9$. The character sums are perfectly healthy over the ring.

\begin{proposition}[the discriminator is the character order]\label{prop:scope}
What controls the $+4$ is the order of the generating character, not whether
the coefficient ring is a field.
\end{proposition}

The evidence is a third experiment, chosen to separate the two readings. Let
$R=\FF_3[x]/(x^2)$, a Frobenius ring which is \emph{not} a field --- it has
zero divisors and non-unimodular nonzero vectors --- but whose generating
character $\psi(c)=\zeta_3^{c_1}$ (the socle coordinate) has order $3$. Then
$|R|=9$ and $\vlam(9)=4$, exactly as over $\FF_9$. Running the same
computation: $\rho$ is a homomorphism, the flat block again satisfies
$F^2+2F-80I=0$ with $\det F=327{,}680{,}000$, and the measured depths are
$\{8,10,12\}$ with minimum $8=\vlam(q)+4$. \textbf{The $+4$ survives over a
non-field.}

So the trichotomy is: $\FF_9$ (field, character order $3$) gives $8$;
$\FF_3[x]/(x^2)$ (not a field, character order $3$) gives $8$; $\ZZ/9$ (not a
field, character order $9$) gives $12$. The field hypothesis plays no role;
Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp} is a statement about coefficient rings whose generating
character has order $p$, and its proof goes through verbatim in that
generality.

The family $R=\FF_p[x]/(x^k)$ --- socle $(x^{k-1})$, generating character
$\psi(c)=\zeta_p^{c_{k-1}}$ of order $p$, $|R|=p^k$ --- realizes this for every
$p$ and $k$, with $k=1$ the field $\FF_p$ and $k\ge2$ a genuine non-field. We
tested it along both axes:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
$R$ & $|R|$ & field? & $\vlam(|R|)$ & predicted & observed min\\
\midrule
$\FF_3[x]/(x^2)$ & $9$  & no & $4$ & $8$  & $8$\\
$\FF_3[x]/(x^3)$ & $27$ & no & $6$ & $10$ & $10$\\
$\FF_5[x]/(x^2)$ & $25$ & no & $8$ & $12$ & $12$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each is checked with a homomorphism-validated representation, the flat-block
quadratic, and the closed-form flat determinant. Commutativity is not needed
either: the Heisenberg cocycle is associative over any ring (the six cross
terms cancel identically) and the centre is central, and the law is confirmed
over two noncommutative Frobenius rings of order $81$ --- the semisimple
$M_2(\FF_3)$ with $\psi(X)=\zeta_3^{\tr X}$, and the \emph{local} twisted dual
numbers $\FF_9[\theta]$, $\theta^2=0$, $\theta a=a^3\theta$, with $\psi$ the
socle character. Both have $\vlam(81)=8$, in both the representation is a
homomorphism and the flat determinant matches Lemma~\ref{lem:flat}, and both
attain minimum depth $12=\vlam(|R|)+4$. The second is the more informative:
being local and nilpotent as well as noncommutative, it tests those features
together, which the semisimple $M_2(\FF_3)$ cannot. The exponents coincide
exactly with those of the \emph{fields} of the same size ($\FF_{27}\to10$,
$\FF_{25}\to12$): \textbf{the law cannot distinguish $\FF_{p^k}$ from
$\FF_p[x]/(x^k)$.} The measured bound $\vlam(\det D)\ge2|R|$ of
Conjecture~\ref{conj:top} also holds over each of them, with equality
($18$, $54$, $50$), so the sole open step behaves identically off the field
locus.

Finally, the hypothesis is \emph{necessary}, not merely convenient. Repeating
the $\ZZ/9$ experiment at a second prime, over $\ZZ/25$ --- generating
character of order $25$, $\vlam(5)=20$, $\vlam(25)=40$, so Newton's divisions
by $5,10,15,20,25$ each cost $20$ against the $4$ they cost over
$\ZZ[\zeta_5]$ --- the law would predict exponent $44$ and the measured
minimum is $30$. So the law fails at both $p=3$ and $p=5$ once the character
order exceeds $p$.

The failed value is \emph{not} a function of $\vlam(q)$: $\ZZ/9$ landed exactly
on $\vlam(q)=12$, whereas $\ZZ/25$ gives $30$, strictly below $\vlam(25)=40$.
A third point resolves the picture. At $\ZZ/27$ the minimum is $36$, and
\[
12,\;30,\;36 \;=\; q+\frac{q}{p} \;=\; p^{\,n-1}(p+1)
\qquad\text{at }\ZZ/p^{n}=\ZZ/9,\ \ZZ/25,\ \ZZ/27 .
\]
So the failure region carries a law of its own --- one agreeing with
$\vlam(q)=n\,p^{n-1}(p-1)$ (namely $12,40,54$) only at $\ZZ/9$, which is
precisely why two data points looked structureless.

\begin{conjecture}
Over $\ZZ/p^{n}$ with $p$ odd, $\min_c\vlam\bigl(\det B_t(c)-\det F\bigr)
=p^{\,n-1}(p+1)$.
\end{conjecture}

\noindent Three data points; a proof would presumably come from tracking which
Newton division dominates the recursion.
